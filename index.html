Acton Water Watch

What lies beneath the storm drain?

Rainfall washes copper from Acton roads into storm drains, and onward to ponds, brooks, and the Assabet River. The principal source is brake-pad wear; roofs and road surfaces contribute less. Dissolved copper is essential in trace amounts but toxic at low concentrations, damaging the gills and sense of smell of fish and other aquatic organisms. This project asks: can bacteria remove dissolved copper from stormwater? Fifteen Acton drains are proposed as sampling sites, and E. coli K-12 (BSL-1) is tested in the laboratory for binding dissolved copper at the cell surface, so that removing the cells removes the copper.

    How to read the copper estimates. Published stormwater medians for total copper are approximately 0.016 mg/L; highway runoff is approximately 0.022–0.054 mg/L, and busy urban runoff can reach approximately 0.25 mg/L. Estimates here (0.02–0.25 mg/L) are scaled by traffic and surrounding land use. K-12 verdicts: reported CuSO4 minimum inhibitory concentrations for E. coli K-12 are approximately 78–312 mg/L in minimal broth and approximately 960 mg/L in rich LB, over 300× above every estimate here, so survival at all 15 sites is plausible. Survival does not establish removal: each verdict requires laboratory confirmation, and pH, flow, and other metals remain unmeasured.

    Collection + methodology

    Two phases. Phase 1 estimates copper at 15 Acton drains with test strips only, read on site during rainfall; nothing is bottled. Phase 2 tests in the laboratory whether E. coli K-12 reduces dissolved copper in prepared solutions, quantified with a spectrophotometer against known standards. Procedures are identical at every site and in every trial.

    1. 1

      Choose the sites

      • 15 sites selected to span land-use types: school, library, station, river, ponds, brooks, residential streets, and the busiest junction in Acton.
      • Traffic volume and land use are the principal drivers of copper in runoff, so the sample maximises contrast between low- and high-input sites.
    2. 2

      Read the drains with strips only, on a rainy day

      • Sampling occurs only during rainfall, when stormwater is flowing at the drain.
      • Test strips only: no samples are bottled or transported.
      • Copper and pH strips are immersed at a fixed depth for a fixed time and read on site.
      • An identical procedure at every site ensures differences reflect the site, not the method. pH is recorded because it controls how much copper remains dissolved.
    3. 3

      Read each strip against its scale

      • Strip colour intensity increases with copper concentration and is matched to the printed scale.
      • Strips must resolve to 0.05 mg/L: estimated site concentrations are 0.02–0.25 mg/L, and a scale starting at 0.3 mg/L would return zero at most sites.
      • Two readings are taken per site; both are recorded.
      • Strip values are semi-quantitative bands. They feed the graph (step 5) and set the lab levels (step 8); they are not final concentrations.
    4. 4

      Record the conditions

      • For each site: location, GPS coordinates, date, and time.
      • Weather and antecedent rainfall are recorded, as runoff copper varies strongly with storm history.
    5. 5

      Graph it: busyness against copper

      • Busyness is scored on the dirtiness scale: one score, 1–5, per site, used for both. A wooded site (Nagog Pond, the Arboretum) scores 1; the busiest junction scores 5.
      • Strip copper (mg/L) is plotted against this score and a least-squares regression line (LSRL) is fitted.
      • The slope tests the hypothesis that higher-traffic, more built-up sites produce higher drain copper.
      • Points far from the line are flagged for rechecking; the fitted equation gives predicted copper for any score.
      • Published basis: brake wear contributes approximately 47% of the copper load in urban runoff (Davis et al., 2001).
    6. 6

      Pin the number down with the spectrophotometer

      • A copper-specific bicinchoninate reagent (Hach CuVer) binds dissolved copper and forms a purple complex; colour intensity rises with concentration.
      • Absorbance is read at approximately 560 nm.
      • Concentration is derived from a calibration curve of known standards treated identically (Beer–Lambert, A = mC + b).
      • This instrument is used only for the laboratory test (steps 7–8); no drain water is bottled for it.
    7. 7

      Separate the bacteria before reading: centrifuge, then pipette

      • After contact, each tube is centrifuged at approximately 4,000 rpm for 5 minutes to pellet the cells.
      • Only the clear supernatant is pipetted off, without disturbing the pellet; reagent is added to the supernatant.
      • No syringe filter is used: a membrane can retain copper and imitate removal.
      • The result is dissolved copper remaining, excluding copper bound to the cells.
    8. 8

      The separate K-12 test: four concentrations, two groups

      • Solutions are prepared from CuSO4 in distilled water and buffer at pH 5.5–6.0, without LB or stormwater, so no other component binds copper.
      • Four concentrations, fixed after strip sampling: the lowest, middle, and highest drain readings, plus one higher laboratory level.
      • Group A: copper only (control). Group B: live E. coli K-12 + copper; culture adjusted to OD600 1.0 and washed twice in buffer.
      • Contact is approximately 20 hours at room temperature, identical for both groups.
      • Design: 4 concentrations × 2 groups × 3 replicates = 24 tubes, plus 1 reagent blank and 4 standards (0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 mg/L) = 29 readings.
      • A concentration range shows whether removal persists or declines as cell binding sites saturate.
    9. 9

      Calculate % removal, and check it means something

      • % removal = (C0 − Cf) / C0 × 100, where C0 is initial and Cf final dissolved copper.
      • Each condition uses 3 independent replicates; results are reported as mean ± standard deviation.
      • Removal is claimed only where Group B falls consistently below Group A across replicates and the difference is statistically significant. Overlap between groups is treated as no effect.
    10. 10

      Two groups only, and why the control matters

      • The two-group design fits the available after-school schedule.
      • Group A quantifies abiotic loss (precipitation, adsorption to tube walls). Without it, such loss could be misattributed to the bacteria.
      • No filter group is included, as no filter is used. No heat-killed group is included, so surface binding and active uptake are not separated: the outcome is total removal by live cells relative to the control.
      • All tubes share volume, temperature, location, and contact time; caps remain sealed. pH is checked before and after in one tube per group, as pH drift can precipitate copper.
    11. 11

      The lab plan: four after-school days

      • Day 1: streak K-12 from school stock onto LB agar; incubate overnight.
      • Day 2: inoculate one colony into 200 mL LB broth; shake at 37°C overnight.
      • Day 3: adjust the culture to OD600 1.0 in buffer, wash cells twice, prepare the 24 tubes, and leave approximately 20 hours at room temperature.
      • Day 4: centrifuge, pipette the supernatant, add reagent, and read all tubes against the standards and blank.
      • Two substitutions are stated in the methods: overnight culture diluted to OD600 1.0 in place of a log-phase culture, and approximately 20 hours of contact in place of 4 hours, applied equally to both groups.

    Recording the data

    • Every reading is recorded at the time of measurement. Tube ID = group – concentration – replicate (e.g. B-0.5-2).
    • For each of the 29 tubes, the notebook records: pH before and after, OD600, absorbance at 560 nm, copper from the calibration curve, and % removal.
    • The calibration curve is plotted and the reagent blank checked (approximately zero) before sample values are accepted.
    • Strip results are recorded as the scale step shown (0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 1, 2 ppm). A reading of 0 is recorded as below the detection floor, not as zero copper.

    Taking the strip readings right

    • Sample only during active storm flow, in flowing water at the drain (EPA stormwater sampling guidance); never in puddles or ponds.
    • Target at least 3 dry days before sampling, so copper re-accumulates on road surfaces; record the date of the last rainfall.
    • Wear powder-free gloves and do not touch the reactive pad. Keep dip time, read time, and lighting constant: a late read overstates colour.
    • Run one field blank (strip in distilled water) before the first drain; it must read zero.
    • One record sheet per outing: site, date, time, weather, both copper readings, and pH.
    • Context: the EPA freshwater copper benchmark is 0.005 mg/L. Even the lowest Acton estimate, 0.02 mg/L, exceeds it.

    Why one lab level runs higher than the drains

    • Three levels match the drain readings; the fourth, approximately 1.0 mg/L, exceeds every drain estimate.
    • Vulpe et al. (2023): E. coli remained viable in copper up to 2.5 mM, and the best-performing strain accumulated 4.79 mg/L. K-12 is a different strain, but 1.0 mg/L is within a workable range.
    • Ortiz et al. (2025): total copper in a stormwater pond reached only 0.05 mg/L over one year; dissolved copper was 0.0004–0.0064 mg/L, at or below reliable assay range.
    • The higher level therefore guarantees a trustworthy spectrophotometer reading, while the drain-matched levels test performance at realistic concentrations.

    What the lab number can and cannot say

    • The lab test measures total dissolved copper removed from clean buffer water, where copper is mostly free Cu2+.
    • Nason et al. (2012) found that in real highway stormwater, over 99.9% of dissolved copper was bound to organic matter.
    • In that study, free copper stayed under 6.3 ng/L even when total dissolved copper was 2 to 20 µg/L.
    • The lab test is therefore a worst case for the cells: they face more free copper in the lab than they would in a real drain.
    • The test strips and the CuVer assay both read total copper, not the free part.
    • This project does not measure free copper separately.
    • pH is held at 5.5 to 6.0 in every tube, because how copper splits between free and bound forms shifts with pH.

    What can go wrong, and how the controls catch it

    • pH drift. Copper can precipitate above pH 6 and look like removal. I measure pH before and after every tube and hold the buffer at pH 5.5 to 6.0. If the pH moved, I note it and read the control first.
    • Copper sticking to tube walls. Copper lost to the plastic looks like removal. Group A sits in the same tube type for the same 20 hours, so any loss in A is wall loss, not bacteria. All 29 tubes use one tube type.
    • Evaporation overnight. Volume loss raises the concentration. I mark the liquid level on each tube at the start and keep the tubes capped at one temperature. If a level dropped, I note it.
    • Assay interference. Iron and other metals can bias the bicinchoninate reading. The reagent blank and the standards are made in the same buffer and read at 560 nm. If the blank is not near zero, I stop and re-make the reagents.
    • Cells settling or clumping. OD600 drifts if tubes sit still. I mix each tube the same way before reading and record the OD600 actually seen, not the target value.
    • Contamination. One stray organism can change a tube. The blank and Group A give me the comparison, and each condition runs in triplicate. If one replicate sits far from the other two, I re-read that tube before deciding anything. I never silently drop a reading: I report all three values, plus the mean and standard deviation.
    • What the tubes do. Of the 29 tubes, 24 are test tubes: 4 concentrations, 2 groups, 3 replicates each. The other 5 are the reagent blank and 4 calibration standards at 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 ppm.
    • Group A, copper only, is the baseline for wall loss and precipitation. Group B, live K-12 plus copper, is the test.
    • The reagent blank is the zero. The 4 standards make the calibration curve. Three replicates give a mean and a standard deviation, so one odd tube cannot carry the result.
    • Rensing and Grass (2003) describe three copper-handling systems in K-12. CopA pumps copper out of the cytoplasm. CueO and CusCFBA guard the periplasm. My test levels sit far below the published tolerance range, so the cells stay alive through the 20-hour contact.
    • The number I measure is net removal, copper bound or held by the cells, because the cells also pump copper back out.
